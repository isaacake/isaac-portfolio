Overview
The design challenge was significant: a healthcare worker's relationship with shift work is stressful, time-sensitive and often compliance-driven. The platform needed to feel trustworthy and efficient without feeling clinical or bureaucratic. A unified design system, especially a clear, purposeful colour language, was the foundation of that goal.

What shipped is a token-based system live in Figma and code. Claude gave the build speed, but every architectural call stayed mine.

✦The Challenge

Background

Pulse is the mobile workforce platform built by Acacium Group, one of the largest healthcare staffing agencies in the UK and ANZ. The app serves agency workers: nurses, educators, carers and allied health professionals who need to manage variable shifts, availability windows, compliance paperwork and timesheets, all from a single interface.

The platform needed to feel trustworthy and efficient without feeling clinical or bureaucratic.

Understanding the problem

I began by hosting a kickoff session to understand the background behind the service. The session brought together UX, PMs, marketing and delivery to make sure everyone was on the same page. The key questions I wanted answered were:

  1. What is the problem?
  2. Why does the problem exist?
  3. Who would be the target audience?
  4. Where is this service focused?
  5. When does the website need to be released?

✦The Solution

Setting up the core foundation

Two things had to be true for this system to succeed:

01

Decide once, not fifty times

Foundations that make a decision once, so every screen inherits it instead of re-solving it.

02

Live beyond Figma

A system that exists in code as well as in Figma, so design and build never drift apart.

✦Visual Design

A purposeful colour language

Colour carried the most weight. Deep navy and teal give the app a calm, trustworthy base, the sky blue keeps it warm and approachable, and a small set of status colours tells a worker at a glance where every shift and timesheet stands.

✦Key Features

The home screen greets the worker by name, counts down to the next shift, and puts the shift details and a one-tap Clock in right at the top, with shortcuts to find shifts and timesheets.

A monthly calendar with a clear Available, Booked and Unavailable legend. Workers add time windows, edit or remove them, and select several days at once so they get matched to shifts that suit them.

Upcoming, Completed and Applied tabs, filters and sorting, and shift cards that show role, date, centre, distance and hours at a glance, so workers can pick the shifts they want.

✦Technical Pipeline

Getting tokens out of Figma

Once the visual foundations were set, the system had to become deployable code. Before this, updating a token meant copying values from Figma into code by hand, with no way to know if a file had drifted until something looked wrong.

  1. FigmaVariables and components
  2. TokensPrimitive and semantic layers
  3. CodeMirrored in the repo
  4. StorybookDocumented for the team

✦AI Workflow

Working with Claude as a creative partner, and catching it where it drifts

Claude is only as useful as the context you give it. Before asking for anything, I fed it six files, covering:

Project instructionsPersonasDesign principlesPlatform constraintsDesign system best practices

With the Figma MCP connected, I built the foundations in order: colour, typography, spacing, radius, elevation, layout. Every semantic token got a written description covering when to use it and why.

Claude Code ran in parallel on the repo, reading across the codebase, editing files, and keeping Figma and code aligned as the system grew.

Benchmarking did the job context alone couldn't

I kept three systems open through the entire build:

Nord Health

Healthcare-specific reasoning

Atlassian

Scale and token architecture

Polaris

Its staged component model

That running comparison is what told me when to push back on Claude's output instead of taking it as given.

✦Outcome

Where the system stands today

Primitive and semantic tokens are live in Figma and mirrored in code. The foundation the product needed from the start now exists, and every new screen starts from a decision instead of a guess.

Accessibility is encoded at the token level

Accessibility used to be a checklist at the end. Now it's built into the token layer itself. If a colour pairing fails WCAG 2.2 AA, it isn't in the semantic layer to begin with, so there's nothing failing to catch later.

Documentation that explains

The documentation is built in Storybook and explains what each token is for and when not to use it, so extending the system doesn't mean re-arguing decisions that are already made.

The next test is putting it in front of someone else: a designer extending the system or a developer implementing it.

✦Reflections

AI gives speed, but judgment is mine

Output still needs checking

Claude built the structure fast. Whether it was any good took checking it against systems that already worked.

Learning while building

I learned design systems by working through naming conventions and token structure, asking Claude when I was unsure.

TegaPay is a mobile banking redesign for iOS: a ground-up rethink of how people interact with their money.

I was brought in in Summer 2026 to lead the design of their MVP. When TegaPay approached me, they had an idea of what they wanted to build, but nothing solidified, and a tight deadline of two months to have the designs completed.

The goal was to get a working prototype to show investors, raise funding and test with initial users.

✦The Challenge

The vision: banking, made simpler

Most mobile banking apps were designed by committees who optimised for feature parity rather than user clarity. The result is interfaces that leave users anxious instead of informed.

Pinpointing the issues

  1. Crammed widgets competing for attention on every screen
  2. Buried navigation that hides everyday tasks
  3. No meaningful hierarchy, so nothing feels important
  4. Anxious users who feel unsure instead of informed
A person should be able to check their balance, send money to a contact and understand their spending, all within 60 seconds of opening the app.

✦Research

Understanding the people behind the brief

With two months and an idea that wasn't solidified yet, research had to be quick and focused. I started from the founders' vision and the 60-second brief, looked closely at how everyday banking apps handle the same jobs, and turned what we learned into a clear picture of who TegaPay was for.

Who we designed for

EU

  • Pay people fast
  • Know where the money went
Frustrations

  • Transfers hidden in menus
  • Fees discovered too late
Needs
  • A clear hierarchy
  • Quick actions up front
  • Confidence before sending

Where existing banking apps fall short

Pain point A

Interfaces crammed with widgets

Feature parity wins over clarity, so the balance and the next action compete with everything else on screen.

Pain point B

Buried navigation

Everyday tasks like sending money or finding a card sit several taps deep, behind menus that change from screen to screen.

Pain point C

Anxiety instead of information

Without hierarchy or clear confirmations, people aren't sure what they're paying, what it costs, or where their money went.

✦The Challenge

✦Key Sections

Here are a few key parts of the page I designed, and the thinking behind each one.

01 · Making the hero show the product, not describe it

The job in one line, with a real product shot

✦

Above the fold: 2 calls to action, 3 trust signals and 1 product shot.

02 · Making prices feel live instead of listing them

I turned live prices into their own section, built around what a holder checks first when they open the app: where the market is right now. Five cards, one per major asset, each show the current price, the 24-hour change and a small sparkline for the day's movement.

The headline promises prices that keep up with you, and the subline explains how: quotes streamed from every major venue and normalised into one book, so the number on the card matches the number in your portfolio and at checkout.

Below the cards, a stats strip backs up the scale behind it: $4.2B+ in assets tracked, 3M+ investors, 500+ connected exchanges and wallets, and 99.98% sync uptime. If the copy said the data was reliable, the section had to show the proof.

✦

Every claim had to be backed by something visible. If the copy said the numbers agree, the interface next to it had to show those numbers.

03 · Answering the trust question head-on

In crypto, the biggest objection is the fear of losing funds. I gave security its own section with a direct headline, "We can read. We can never move.", backed by read-only keys, AES-256 encryption at rest, SOC 2 Type II controls and 24/7 monitoring.

Just above it, three simple steps set expectations:

  1. ConnectLink your exchanges and wallets
  2. ReconcileVestra rebuilds your full history
  3. See it, file itOne clear picture and a tax report

04 · Putting the product in their hand, not just in the copy

This section is rebuilt around a real screen. On the left, the pitch: everything you need to run a crypto platform in one app, supported by four short cards covering trading flows, launch speed, multi-chain wallets and security. On the right, the wallet view as a user would see it on their phone.

The screen was chosen to prove the copy, not decorate it. It shows a total portfolio value with the 24-hour change, a chart with time ranges from one day to all time, an allocation breakdown by asset, and an insight that flags BTC as the largest holding at 54.98% before it crosses a concentration limit.

The cards say what the product does; the phone shows it already doing it. Two clear actions close the section: get started, or explore every feature in depth.

✦Details

Sweating the details that build confidence

Throughout the page, I kept coming back to one principle from the design system:

The number is the interface.

If people can see it, they're more likely to believe it. Here's how that showed up across the page:

  • Stats and live prices set in a monospaced face so digits line up
  • Every price change marked with a sign and a caret, not colour alone
  • Trust signals placed directly under the main call to action
  • Pricing stated plainly: free while your portfolio is under $10,000
  • An FAQ that opens on the hardest question: "Can Vestra move my funds?"

✦Visual Design

Calm, dark and numerically honest

Deep green-black surfaces keep a busy market feeling calm, a single Vestra green carries every action, and gains and losses are always paired with a sign and a caret so colour is never the only signal.

✦Reflections

Start from the user's questions, not a template

✦The Challenge

Finding a home in Lagos is built on guesswork

Buyers and renters piece together listings from scattered sources, can't easily tell which homes are genuine or fairly priced, and rarely know who to trust once they find one they like. Agents cover the whole city, so local knowledge gets lost.

Pinpointing the issues

  1. Too much noise from listings spread across the city
  2. Unclear value: is this property worth the price?
  3. No local expert who really knows the street
  4. Hard next steps from liking a home to seeing it
Most Lagos agents sell everywhere and know nowhere. We built the opposite.

Where the experience breaks down

Pain point A

Agents who know nowhere

Most agents cover the whole city, so buyers rarely get honest, street-level advice on whether a property is worth it.

Pain point B

No sense of the neighbourhood

Listings show the house but not the area: security, lifestyle, commute and how prices compare.

Pain point C

Too many steps to a viewing

Going from liking a listing to standing inside it means calls, messages and waiting, so good homes get missed.

✦The Solution

Design the whole journey around the neighbourhood and the specialist

Instead of a generic listings site, every part of the experience points back to a neighbourhood and the person who knows it best.

01

Search by neighbourhood

Start from where you want to live, with popular searches and every area one tap away.

02

Decide with confidence

Clear prices, key facts and neighbourhood guides help people judge a home before they visit.

03

Talk to the specialist

Message, call or video-call the local expert, then book a viewing in a few taps.

✦Key Features

01

A warm first impression

Onboarding opens on a real home and a simple promise: "A place to call home." The homepage gets straight to work with "Find a place worth coming home to.", a neighbourhood search and featured listings.

02

Search that starts from the neighbourhood

People search by area, property type or budget, switch between sale and rent, and pick from popular searches like "3 bed in Lekki". Filters live in a bottom sheet with location, type, bedrooms, a price range and amenities, and the button tells you exactly how many properties match.

03

Details that help people decide

Each property leads with large photos, the price in naira, its location and key facts at a glance, with "Contact agent" and "Schedule a viewing" always at the bottom of the screen. Booking takes one screen: pick a day, a time and add your details.

04

Neighbourhood guides and specialists

Every area has its own guide, like Ikoyi's "Quiet luxury in the heart of Lagos.", with properties, average price, specialists and why people live there. Specialist profiles show live listings, deals closed and years with CW, so you know who you're dealing with.

05

Talk to someone who knows the street

Messaging feels familiar, with shared listings and voice notes inside the chat. Audio and video calls make virtual viewings possible, so a buyer can walk through a home without crossing the city.

✦Visual Design

Editorial, warm and quietly premium

A serif display face gives CW the feel of a considered brand rather than a listings portal, paired with a clean sans for everything practical. Warm off-white surfaces, near-black actions and generous photography let the homes do the talking.
